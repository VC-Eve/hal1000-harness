# An instruction that fights its own input loses

Six versions of the same mistake now. In the first three a rule was added to compensate for data
that should not have been there, and the rule lost. In the last three the rule did worse than lose:
it became the answer. Cases 1 and 2 were found tuning Vision; 3, 4 and 5 were found by a user
reading HAL's replies and saying they were wrong.

## Case 1: the timestamps

Cycle summaries went to the model as lines stamped with a wall-clock time:

```
10:29:37 PM: No person is visible. The setting is indoors.
10:29:51 PM: No person is visible. The setting is indoors.
```

The prompt said *"do not remark on the timestamps or on the passage of time itself."* The model
replied:

> "In frame 10:29:37 PM and in frame 10:29:51 PM there is no person..."

Strengthening the prohibition was the obvious next move and the wrong one. The times were in the
prompt because the renderer put them there, and the summariser has no use for them — order is all
it needs, and the feed entry already carries the real timestamp. Numbering the lines removed the
temptation, and the next run produced *"Nothing changed. Two periods have now run, and neither shows
a person."*

```ts
// Numbered rather than timestamped. Wall-clock times were an invitation the
// prompt then had to forbid.
const lines = batch.map((o, i) => `${i + 1}. ${o.caption}`).join("\n");
```

## Case 1b: the ordinals — the same mistake, made while fixing the first one

Numbering the lines fixed the timestamps. It also introduced labels, and the prompt gained a rule
against referring to them: *"the descriptions are numbered in the order they were taken. Do not refer
to their numbers."*

> "Nothing changed. Frame 1 showed nothing. Frame 2 repeated that and then told me the setting is
> indoors, which is a new report rather than a restatement."

Written *after* this document existed, by someone who had just written it. The rule below says stop
supplying the thing first, and the fix reached for a prohibition instead — because the numbers felt
necessary, and they were not. Order is carried by line order.

The lines now carry no label at all. Anything you label invites being referred to by the label, and
a rule against that loses to the label itself.

## Case 2: the counts

A captioner described the same stationary five-blade ceiling fan as having three blades, then four,
then five, across identical frames. The summariser downstream read that variance as an event and
reported the fan changing.

No prompt rule on the *summariser* can fix this: it cannot tell an invented count from an observed
one, because it never sees the image. The fix belongs upstream, in what the captioner is asked for —
*"Do not give exact counts of objects."* Nothing downstream needed those numbers, so the cheapest
repair was to stop generating them.

Note what survived: "one person or several" is still asked for, because that distinction carries
real meaning and does not wobble the way "five blades" does. The rule is not *ask for less*, it is
*do not ask for precision the model cannot deliver and the reader does not need.*

That fix was itself written as a prohibition, and case 3 below is the bill for it. The requirement
was right and the form was wrong; it now reads "give rough quantities rather than exact counts of
objects", which asks for the same thing without putting "do not" in the model's context.

## The rule

When a model keeps doing something you have forbidden, check whether your own input is inviting it.
A prompt rule and the data it contradicts are in a fight, and the data usually wins — especially with
a small local model, where every additional rule competes for limited instruction-following.

Ask in this order:

1. **Can I stop supplying the thing?** Cheapest and most reliable. No rule to obey, no rule to break.
2. **Can I stop asking for it upstream?** When the noise is generated by an earlier stage, fix it
   there — a later stage cannot distinguish signal from noise it did not witness.
3. **Only then, add a prompt rule** — and expect it to hold less well than the two above.

## Cases 3, 4 and 5: the prohibition becomes the answer

Three more, all in one session, and all a step worse than the first three. In cases 1 and 2 a rule
lost to the data. In these, the rule *became the output*.

**The caption prompt.** It carried three prohibitions, one of which read "Do not guess at motion,
lighting, states you cannot see". Four captions in ten came back as "I'm sorry, but I can't see
anything" — one of them adding "I'm just a text model". The frames were valid JPEGs of about 42KB
that the same model, on the same machine, described in full detail under a positive prompt. The
prompt talked the model out of having eyes.

**A gloss on a number.** A percentage was reaching a conversation bare, so it was given its unit: "a
percentage is how strongly that face matched, nothing more". Meant as a definition, read as a
caution. The model escalated it into a rule it invented — "do not read it as a record of who is
sitting here now" — and then obeyed that against its own data, refusing to identify someone it had
recognised without a break for two minutes, well above the statement threshold. Told directly "that's
me at my desk", it answered "I am not assuming anything about the person in front of me".

**A scope limit on a description.** "Speak about them only as far as what you saw supports" is right
for narration, whose whole job is commenting on people from what a camera saw. Carried into chat it
applied to every sentence about a person — their work, their week, their code — and made ordinary
conversation stilted.

## The sharper rule

A prohibition is a topic. Naming a thing the model must not do puts that thing in the context, and a
small model reaches for what is in the context.

So: **say what the material is, or what to do with it. Never what it is not, or what not to do.**

- Not "this percentage does not mean X" → supply the evidence and let the band decide.
- Not "do not describe what you cannot see" → "describe what the picture shows".
- Not "do not report on this context" → "this is my own sight, held the way I hold knowing where I
  am". That framing was measured working: unrelated questions stopped drawing room reports.

Two corollaries this session paid for:

**A rule that is right in one place is not right in every place.** The same sentence protected
narration and damaged chat. Scope it rather than sharing it.

**Explaining a field is itself a prohibition risk.** The instinct to clarify — "this means X, nothing
more" — is the same shape. If a value needs a caveat to be safe, the fix is usually not to send the
value.

## How to prove it, rather than guess

The caption failure was diagnosed by elimination in about ten minutes, and each step ruled out a
whole class:

1. **Is the input bad?** Compared retained frames behind refusals against successes — byte size, SOI
   and EOI markers. Identical. Not the frames.
2. **Is it nondeterminism?** Sent one frame sixteen times. Zero refusals. Not flakiness.
3. **Is it the server?** A/B'd `cache_prompt` on and off, eight each. Zero refusals both ways. Not
   prompt caching.
4. **Is it the prompt?** Sent the frames that *had* refused, with the prompt in use: three or four
   refusals out of four each. Then swapped only the prompt across the same ten frames: four refusals
   became zero.

The retained frames and the inference log are what made this possible — the log records which frame
produced which caption, so a past failure can be replayed exactly. Keeping the input beside the
output is what turns "the model is being weird" into a bisect.

## Where this bites hardest

Multi-stage pipelines where one model's output becomes another's input. The second stage sees only
text and must take it at face value, so anything the first stage fabricates or wobbles becomes fact
downstream. Every guardrail you can move upstream is worth more than the same guardrail written
downstream.
