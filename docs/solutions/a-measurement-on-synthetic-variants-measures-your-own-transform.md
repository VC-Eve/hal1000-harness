## Context

The face recogniser needed a similarity threshold. A spike measured it: the same person, embedded
twice, scored **0.93**, while a crop that was not a face scored **0.21**. Wide, clean gap. Appearance
continuity was set at 0.75 on the strength of it, comfortably inside the band.

Running the real loop, one person sitting still produced **seventeen appearances in forty seconds**,
and a single observation read *"someone who looks like SW and someone who looks like SW and someone
who looks like SW and someone who looks like SW"*. Genuinely independent captures of one person,
seconds apart, score **0.53 to 0.78** — not 0.93.

The 0.93 was not wrong arithmetic. It was measured on **variants synthesised from a single captured
frame**: the frame rotated 12°, scaled 0.85, shifted 25px. Every variant shared the same expression,
the same lighting, the same instant of the same face. What that number actually measured was *the
embedder's invariance to rotation and scale* — a real property, and not the property the threshold
needed.

The gap between the two is the entire distance between "this works" and a feature that reproduced the
exact defect it was designed to prevent.

## Guidance

### Name what a measurement is over, not just what it produced

"Same-person similarity is 0.93" is not a finding. "Same-person similarity across synthetic
rotations of one frame is 0.93" is, and it invites the obvious next question — what about across two
real captures? Write the provenance next to the number, in the same sentence. A number whose
provenance has been dropped will be reused in a context it was never valid for, and nothing about it
will look suspicious.

### One real pair beats a hundred synthetic ones

Synthetic variants are attractive because they are deterministic, cheap, and produce a tidy spread.
They are worth having — the geometry tests built on them are still the fastest way to catch a
mirrored warp. But they cannot tell you the size of the natural variation, because they contain none
of it. Before a constant depends on a measured range, take **at least one pair from genuinely
independent capture**, however awkward. Two photographs, seconds apart, would have shown 0.53 here.

### Suspect any range that is suspiciously clean

Real same-person similarity was 0.53–0.78: a wide, messy band with its floor uncomfortably close to
where different-person scores are expected. The synthetic measurement produced 0.93 against 0.21 — a
gap so comfortable it should have prompted the question "what variation am I not capturing?" A
measurement that makes a hard problem look easy is usually measuring an easier problem.

### Bake the measured range into the test, with its provenance

The fix is not just a better constant; it is a test that fails if someone raises the bar back:

```
// Measured on the real loop: independent captures of one person, seconds
// apart, score 0.53 to 0.78. A bar above that floor fragmented a single
// visit into seventeen appearances.
expect(CONTINUITY_THRESHOLD).toBeLessThan(0.8);
expect(CONTINUITY_THRESHOLD).toBeGreaterThan(0.4);
```

Constants derived from measurement decay silently, because nothing in the code remembers where they
came from. The test is where the provenance survives a refactor.

### Prefer a structural signal over a tuned number

The durable fix was not a better threshold. It was consulting the gallery per face per frame: two
different people cannot both be the same enrolled person, so a face matching P continues the
appearance already resolved to P regardless of how far the embedding drifted. That holds without
depending on any number being right.

When a constant is being tuned for the second time, that is the signal to look for a signal that
does not need tuning.

## Why This Matters

The wrong number did not produce an obviously broken feature. It produced one that worked in tests,
worked in the spike, and failed in a way that looked like a different bug entirely — enrolment
refusing, one person appearing as several, duplicate person records accumulating. Three separate
user-visible symptoms, all downstream of a measurement that was taken correctly on the wrong thing.

It also cost twice. The number was corrected once from 0.75 to 0.62-with-overlap, and *that* was
still tuned against a partly-synthetic understanding; it took the live loop again to reach a
structural fix.

## When to Apply

- Any spike whose output is a number a constant will later depend on.
- Any test fixture generated by transforming a captured sample rather than capturing again.
- Any threshold, tolerance, or cutoff — the whole point of one is to sit inside a measured range, so
  a wrong range is a wrong feature.
- Before writing "measured" in a document. The word carries authority the reader cannot audit.

## Examples

**The claim as originally written**, in the brief's Measured Constraints — accurate, and read as more
than it was:

> Two independent captures scored 0.92 on one run and 0.61 on another.

That sentence was about the *bounding-box crop* baseline and was honest. The synthetic 0.93 that
followed the landmark warp was then quietly treated as the same kind of number, and it was not.

**The limitation was even written down** in the sidecar's own residuals file at ship time:

> The discrimination comparison uses synthetic variants, not two real captures. Rotating and scaling
> one photograph is not the same as a person turning their head.

It was recorded, and then a constant depended on the number anyway. Writing the caveat is not the
same as acting on it — if a limitation would change a constant, it belongs in the test that guards
that constant, not only in prose.

where the measurement is fine but the thing measured is not yours.
